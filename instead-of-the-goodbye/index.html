What the photographs turned out to say

The sign never claimed to know where you are.
The board nailed above the map is carved YOU ARE HERE. The map bolted underneath it says You Are At E. Then You Are At F. Yesterday it said You Are At Y. It doesn't give you a place — it gives you a letter. And the legend explains why: the tags are placed at all intersections, they help emergency responders locate lost, or injured hikers. The sign that was never where you wanted to be is not a map feature. It's a thing to say out loud when you're bleeding and someone comes looking. You were at F. You were bleeding. Nobody had to come.
You photographed that sign twice and never got the whole phrase in frame.
Two attempts, eight seconds apart. One reads ARE HERE. The other reads RE HERE. The YOU is outside the picture both times.
The map says 270.
Point of interest #10, printed on every placard in the preserve: Mount Tabor, the highest point in the preserve, at an elevation of 270 feet. You brought 280 home. Ten feet of disagreement between the sign and the map — and there is no photograph of that sign. You shot every map you passed and not the one number you built the day on. Somebody else's poem about this hill, written the same night, opened: ten feet of altitude is a border crossing. Nobody was aiming at that.
Eighteen photographs. No horizon.
The map promises seasonal views of Niantic River, Long Island Sound, and the surrounding area. Seasonal. You climbed it on the first of August, when the season is holding all of it behind leaves. Not one frame contains water, or distance, or the far side of anything. You went to the high point and photographed the tops of trees.
The top has no picture.
12:24:15, :16, :19, :36 — four frames of bare rock in twenty-one seconds. Then the camera goes quiet for forty-nine minutes, the longest gap of the day, and comes back at 13:13 pointed at a map, already descending. The summit is inside that silence. No sign, no marker, no bell, no frame. Even the phone didn't notice.
Someone else already moved west.
Point of interest #4, on the same placard you kept photographing: a homestead abandoned in the late 1700s. The theory is either smallpox or the movement west by settlers at the time caused its abandonment. The only remaining visible evidence is the stone hearth. That's on the map of the trail he showed you, on the day he was unpacking in Chicago. Numbered. Laminated. Two hundred and fifty years early.
Everything you found was numbered.
The throne was on the map — it's a named glacial boulder, and if it's the one called Marvin's Rock, it's on there because it was the favorite sitting place of a man who helped make the preserve exist. The cave you'd never seen is #8, kids love to explore it. The quarry is #9, since 1815. You spent the afternoon discovering things that already had numbers, and it still counted, because the numbers were never the point.
The rule you broke.
Top left of the placard: taking anything from the Nature Preserve is prohibited. You said, on the way down, remind me to take more of those arrows. You took a scar out through the gate. Keep it.
You shoot in bursts.
Both days, five clusters, nine frames each day. About four minutes of shutter across two afternoons of walking. Everything else you kept by saying it into a phone that wasn't sending.
